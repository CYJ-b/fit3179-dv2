Coastal Tourism - The Recovery From COVID-19 And Its Current State

As COVID-19 came into the picture, tourism in Australia (including coastal) dropped as a whole. Later, as the borders open up and the number of COVID cases reduced, tourism regions across Australia saw a recovery.

However, how much has coastal tourism recovered? How is it compared to inland tourism? And how much does it contribute to Australia's economy?

1. The Period The World Stopped Travelling.

As the COVID-19 pandemic hit in 2020-2021, the number of visitors across the globe reduced significantly, This number almost fully recovered in 2025 (8,373,250 in 2019 vs 7,822,290 in 2025) with some origin countries having more visits while some still having less visits.

The decrease in international visitors to Australia in 2020-2021 was likely due to people wanting to avoid getting sick. Another contributing factor was the closure of the Australian borders which started on 20 March 2020 as a response to the rising number of cases and deaths across the world, thus further extending the decrease. It was not until 21 February 2022 that the borders fully reopened for fully vaccinated individual, following a decreasing number of worldwide deaths.

2. Before Diving In: What Counts As Coastal?

Tourism Research Australia (TRA) divides the country into 76 tourism regions. In this visualisation, a region is considered as coastal when enough of its boundary touches the sea, with the threshold being higher for bigger regions. For example, 18.5% of Australia's Golden Outback's boundary touches sea, but since it is below its threshold of 27.3%, it is considered inland.

3. Where International Visitors Go

Almost every international visit lands on the coastal regions, with Perth, Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane, and Gold Coast being the most popular regions. This seems to hold even across the years even during the pandemic. This suggests that most international visitors prefer visiting the coastal regions.

This preference is further proven by how the number international visits to the coast has recovered greater and faster than the inland, indicating that coastal regions play a more significant role for Australia's international tourism industry.

4. When And Why They Come

The number of visits for the top 20 busiest coastal seems to follow general seasonal trends, where most visits seem to happen during the summer. This suggests that perhaps most international visitors are visiting coastal regions to visit the beaches, which is fitting for summer.

During the closed borders, visiting friends and relatives briefly became the biggest reason for visits to Australia, overtaking holiday as a reason. Furthermore, "visiting friends and relatives" also had the quickest recovery after the opening of the borders. Other than that, the patterns for the main reasons seem to not have changed much when comparing after vs before the pandemic.

Comparing 2025 to 2019, international visitors seem to prefer longer stays compared to having short 1-7 night stays, especially "31 or more nights" which seems to have the highest increase in share for both coastal and inland tourism regions.

5. Where The Businesses Are

In 2025, the coast is 35% of the land but holds 88% of tourism businesses, 14 times denser than the inland regions. Big cities such as Melbourne has around 21 businesses per km² and Sydney 11, while central regions such as Lasseter have fewer than one per 10,000 km². This highlights the importance of coastal regions as economic drivers compared to inland regions for the tourism industry.

Number of coastal businesses grew by 14,416 (+4.8%) between 2021 and 2025, where sole traders seemingly having the biggest boom and micro businesses having the biggest fall, indicating a shift in the tourism businesses industry in coastal regions.

6. What The Coast Is Worth

The coast holds 35% of the land but 88% of businesses, 93% of international visits, 87% of spend and gross value added (GVA, the value tourism adds to the local economy), and 86% of jobs. This further shows the how coastal tourism contributes more economic value to Australia.

Furthermore, the coastal regions metrics fell further than inland regions in 2020-21 but ultimately climbed higher by 2024-25 for all metrics, especially tourism spendings and the amount of jobs. In addition, the coastal regions' metrics not only bounced back, but rose to higher than before the pandemic. Further highlighting the recovery and potential growth of coastal tourism.

However, the recovery and growth are slightly misleading as most tourism spend are concentrated in only a handful of major coastal regions. Sydney ($36.4bn), Melbourne ($33.4bn), Brisbane ($18.8bn), Perth ($14.7bn) and the Gold Coast ($10.3bn) together make up about 62% of coastal tourism spend in 2024-25. Hence, although there is recovery and growth, they are imbalanced across the coastal regions, with some regions declining instead.

Coastal tourism added $122.4 billion of value in 2024-25. Half of it ($61.2bn) is direct, earned by the tourism industries themselves, such as accommodation ($8.5bn). The other half ($61.2bn) is indirect, earned by the suppliers behind those businesses. This $122.4 billion represents about 4.7% of Australia's all-industry GVA ($2.6 trillion), which in macroeconomics, is a substantial proportion. This further highlights coastal tourism as an economic backbone of Australia.

In addition, Coastal tourism directly supports about 606,000 jobs. Cafes and restaurants are the biggest employer and mostly part-time (181,000 jobs, 65% part-time), highlighting the demand for food from tourists/visitors who are there to enjoy their visit. This 606,000 represents around 4.2% of employed persons in Australia (14,505,000), which is also a substantial proportion. This further shows the importance of coastal tourism in providing jobs and driving the economy of Australia.

7. Conclusion

  • Tourism is a mainly coastal business: 87-93% of visits, spend, jobs, and value added.
  • Most visits from overseas are mainly for holiday, especially during the summer. Additionally, The average length of international stays seem to have increased.
  • Coastal tourism seems to be recovering well from the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, even supassing the values before the pandemic for some metrics (with some caveats that most recovery and growth are focused on the major coastal regions)
  • Coastal tourism contributes a substantial amount of economic value to Australia
