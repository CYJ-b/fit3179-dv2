Coastal Tourism - The Recovery From COVID-19 And Its Current State

As COVID-19 came into the picture, tourism in Australia (including coastal) dropped as a whole. Later, as the borders open and the number of COVID cases reduced, tourism regions across Australia saw a recovery.

However, how much has coastal tourism recovered? How is it compared to inland tourism? And how much does it contribute to Australia's economy?

1. The Period The World Stopped Travelling.

As the COVID-19 pandemic hit in 2020-2021, the number of visitors across the globe reduced significantly. This number almost recovered fully in 2025 (8,373,250 in 2019 vs 7,822,290 in 2025). However, the recovery is uneven with some countries still having fewer visits to Australia compared to 2019.

As the number of deaths and cases increased globally, more people avoided travelling to avoid the sickness, which led to the decrease in international visitors to Australia between 2020-2021. Besides that, another contributing factor was the closure of the Australian borders which started on 20 March 2020.

This border closure was likely a response to the rising number of cases and deaths, and it further extended the decrease in the number of visitors. It was not until 21 February 2022 that the borders fully reopened for fully vaccinated individual, following a decreasing number of worldwide deaths.

2. Before Diving In: What Counts As Coastal?

Tourism Research Australia (TRA) divides the country into 76 tourism regions. In this visualisation, a region is considered as coastal when enough of its boundary touches the sea, with the threshold being higher for bigger regions. For example, 18.5% of Australia's Golden Outback's boundary touches sea, but since it is below its threshold of 27.3%, it is considered inland.

3. Where International Visitors Go

A majority of international visit land on the coastal regions, with Perth, Melbourne, Sydney, Brisbane, and Gold Coast being the most popular regions. This trend was maintained across the years (including during the pandemic), which demonstrates that most international visitors prefer visiting the coastal regions.

This preference is further proven by how the number of international visits to the coast had recovered greater and faster compared to the inland, indicating that coastal regions play a more significant role for Australia's international tourism industry.

4. When And Why They Come

The number of visits for the top 20 busiest coastal seems to follow general seasonal trends, where most visits seem to happen during the summer. This suggests that coastal regions are favoured during summer holidays due to beach-related activities.

During the closed borders, visiting friends and relatives became the biggest reason for visits to coastal regions, overtaking holiday as a reason. Furthermore, "visiting friends and relatives" also had the quickest recovery after the opening of the borders.

Other than that, the patterns for the main reasons seem to not have changed much when comparing after vs before the pandemic.

Comparing 2025 to 2019, international visitors seem to prefer longer stays compared to having short 1-7 night stays, especially "31 or more nights" which seems to have the highest increase in share for both coastal and inland tourism regions.

5. Where The Businesses Are

In 2025, the coast is 35% of the land but holds 88% of tourism businesses, 14 times denser than the inland regions. Big cities such as Melbourne has around 21 businesses per km² and Sydney 11.

On the other hand, central regions such as Lasseter have fewer than one per 10,000 km². This highlights the importance of coastal regions as economic drivers compared to inland regions for the tourism industry.

Number of coastal businesses grew by 14,416 (+4.8%) between 2021 and 2025, where sole traders seemingly having the biggest boom and micro businesses having the biggest fall, indicating a shift in the tourism businesses industry in coastal regions.

6. What The Coast Is Worth

The coast holds 35% of the land but 88% of businesses, 93% of international visits, 87% of spend and gross value added (GVA, the value tourism adds to the local economy), and 86% of jobs. This further shows the how coastal tourism contributes more economic value to Australia.

Furthermore, the coastal regions metrics fell further than inland regions in 2020-21 but ultimately climbed higher by 2024-25 for all metrics, especially tourism spendings and the amount of jobs. In addition, the coastal regions' metrics not only bounced back, but rose to be higher than pre-pandemic. This further highlights the recovery and potential growth of coastal tourism.

However, the overall recovery and growth are slightly misleading as most tourism spend are concentrated in only a handful of major coastal regions. Specifically, Sydney ($36.4bn), Melbourne ($33.4bn), Brisbane ($18.8bn), Perth ($14.7bn) and the Gold Coast ($10.3bn).

Together, they make up about 62% of coastal tourism spend in 2024-25. Hence, although there is recovery and growth, they are imbalanced across the coastal regions, with some regions declining instead.

Coastal tourism added $122.4 billion of value in 2024-25. Half of it ($61.2bn) is direct, earned by the tourism industries themselves, such as accommodation ($8.5bn). The other half ($61.2bn) is indirect, earned by the suppliers behind those businesses.

The $122.4 billion represents about 4.7% of Australia's all-industry GVA ($2.6 trillion), which in macroeconomics, is a substantial proportion. This further highlights coastal tourism as an economic backbone of Australia.

In addition, coastal tourism directly supports about 606,000 jobs. Cafes and restaurants are the biggest businesses (181,000 jobs), highlighting the demand for food from tourists/visitors who are there to enjoy their visit. This creates more part-time opportunities, especially during summer break, as evident by the proportion of part timers (65%) in Cafes and restaurants.

The 606,000 represents around 4.2% of employed persons in Australia (14,505,000), which is also a substantial proportion. This further shows the importance of coastal tourism in providing jobs and driving the economy of Australia.

7. Conclusion

  • Tourism is a mainly coastal business, where across all tourism regions, coastal regions contribute to 87-93% of visits, spend, jobs, and value added.
  • Most visits from overseas are mainly for holiday, and the visit volume is higher during the summer. Additionally, the average length of international visitors' stays seem to have increased.
  • Coastal tourism seems to be recovering well from the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, even supassing the values before the pandemic for some metrics. However, with some caveats that most recovery and growth are focused on the major coastal regions.
  • Coastal tourism contributes a substantial amount of economic value to Australia, in the form of gross value added and employment.
